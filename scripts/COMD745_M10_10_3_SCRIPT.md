# Lecture 10.3 — Speech Recognition and Repair: Clinical Considerations for Care

Recording script.

## Slide 1

This is the last lecture of the course. We are going to take everything you have measured and ask what you do with it in a clinic.

## Slide 2

Here is Mrs. Jones. [pause] Fifty eight years old, referred for a speech evaluation. Her husband understands her at home. Members of her book club frequently ask her to repeat, and she stopped going six months ago. [pause] Her Frenchay assessment shows markedly impaired tongue elevation, markedly to severely impaired alternating tongue movement, markedly impaired palatal function during speech, and markedly impaired respiration for speech. Her laryngeal function is within functional limits. [pause] The two intelligibility rows at the top are blank on purpose. You fill those in from your own lab results. [pause] One thing I want to say plainly. The TORGO database, where these recordings come from, does not publish a diagnosis for individual speakers. So I am not going to tell you what she has, and you should not write one down.

## Slide 3

Notice something about her profile. [pause] It rates her connected speech better than her single words. Most students find that backwards at first, because a sentence has more words in it and therefore more chances to be wrong. [pause] But look at your own data. Your sentence scores were higher than your isolated word scores, on recordings from the same person in the same session. [pause] Her articulation did not change between those two conditions. What changed is how much information was available to you.

## Slide 4

So what does an intelligibility score actually describe. [pause] Not a person. It describes four things at once: a talker, a listener, a response format, and a room. [pause] Your own results prove it. The same nine recordings produced eighty two and a half percent from you and just under forty percent from you, in one sitting, on one afternoon. [pause] A number reported without the conditions attached cannot be compared to anything. Not to another clinic, not to another clinician, and not to the same patient six months later.

## Slide 5

Same problem from another angle. [pause] Back in Module eight you all did the diadochokinetic task. Same instructions, same tool, same five seconds. And the rates across this class covered a wide range. Every one of you has an intact speech system. [pause] A norm describes a sample of people. A baseline describes your patient. [pause] When a patient cannot be meaningfully compared to a norm, and many cannot, you compare them to themselves over time. That is often the better measure anyway.

## Slide 6

Now something about the course itself. [pause] You spent a semester transcribing sounds, and every worksheet asked you to write those sounds down as letters. [pause] Letters are not sounds. English has twenty six letters and roughly forty four sounds, and our spelling hides that gap constantly. [pause] So when you cannot identify a sound, the pull is to write down the nearest word instead. That is exactly what the software does too. It reaches for the closest real word. [pause] Every task in this course was built to interrupt that.

## Slide 7

Here is what I mean, with real numbers. [pause] These are the six stop consonants. The middle column is how often each one occurs in actual conversational speech, from a study of about a hundred thousand phonemes of casual American English. The last column is how often the same six show up as letters on a page. [pause] Look at k. Third most common of these sounds when people talk. Dead last as a letter. [pause] Now look at g. Third most common letter. Least common sound.

## Slide 8

Why do those two disagree so badly? [pause] Take k first. The sound is everywhere. Cat. Come. Cut. Back. School. But look at how we spell it. Almost always with a c. So the sound is common and the letter is rare, because English hands that job to a different letter. [pause] Now g. The letter is everywhere, but a lot of the time it is not making that sound at all. Sign. Though. Gnome. Ginger. Rough. Silent, or something else entirely. [pause] So if you rank these six by how often you see them on a page, you get the wrong answer about what people are actually saying.

## Slide 9

There is one more piece to this, and it is the oldest finding in the module. [pause] Miller and Nicely, nineteen fifty five. They put consonants in noise and measured which features listeners could still recover. [pause] Voicing and nasality held up all the way down to a signal-to-noise ratio of negative twelve decibels. Place of articulation fell apart below positive six. That is a gap of about eighteen decibels. [pause] Put plainly: when a signal degrades, you keep knowing whether a sound was voiced. You lose where in the mouth it was made. Voiced and voiceless stay separate. But p, t, and k start collapsing into each other, and so do b, d, and g.

## Slide 10

Now look at what your class did. [pause] Clip nine, the target was jacket. Two of sixteen got it. The wrong answers were gadgets, gackets, gagints, and cactus. [pause] Look at what those have in common. Every single one keeps the voicing and moves the place. Nobody heard a voiceless sound where the target was voiced. They moved it front to back in the mouth. [pause] That is Miller and Nicely, seventy years later, in sixteen transcriptions from this class. You did not read that paper. You reproduced it.

## Slide 11

Here is how you interrupt it. [pause] Single word testing removes the sentence, so meaning cannot fill the gap for you. Automatic transcription removes meaning entirely, because the software does not have any. And the extended IPA, which is in your worksheet, gives you symbols for productions that are not any word at all. [pause] None of these is more real than conversation. Conversation is the real thing. Each of these tasks just hides one of your crutches from you on purpose, so you can see what is actually in the signal.

## Slide 12

One more idea and then we go clinical. [pause] A word on a page sits still. You can look back at it. It waits for you. [pause] A spoken word does not. Say the word permanence out loud. By the time you get to the end of it, the beginning is already gone, and it has to be gone, or you could not say the rest. Sound only exists while it is disappearing. [pause] But sound does something vision cannot. Rap your knuckles on a box and you can hear whether it is empty. You did not open it. You did not look inside. The sound told you about the interior. [pause] That is why a voice tells you so much about a person. And it is why we can measure the shape of somebody's vocal tract all semester without ever looking inside one.

## Slide 13

That brings us to the feedback link. [pause] It is the arrow on the speech chain that runs from the talker back to the talker. It is how you detect a mismatch between what you meant to say and what actually came out. [pause] Most of that runs below awareness. You correct errors constantly without noticing you made them. That is monitoring, and it is automatic. [pause] Awareness is a separate event, and it does not always happen. A patient whose internal target has drifted can be wrong and feel completely right. [pause] Which matters, because a patient who can hear the mismatch has a reason to work on it. That is where a treatment target comes from.

## Slide 14

Let me give you something practical. [pause] A probe is a short set of untested targets, about ten items, run at the very start of a session. No cues. No models. No reminders. No encouragement. [pause] It will feel deeply uncomfortable to withhold all of that, because your instinct as a clinician is to help. Withhold it anyway. [pause] What a probe tells you is what your patient does without you in the room. That is the only thing that predicts carryover. [pause] And match your probe to whatever task established their baseline, so the two numbers actually mean the same thing when you compare them.

## Slide 15

Here is why I feel strongly about this. [pause] Data you collect with maximum support describes the session. It does not describe the patient. [pause] A goal written around level of cueing is hard to track and does not survive discharge, because on the day they are discharged nobody is cueing them. [pause] Probes are also fast, which matters more than it sounds. New clinicians routinely try to document a session while simultaneously running it, building rapport, planning a home program, and putting in referrals. A ten item probe at the top of the hour spares you that. [pause] Your patient does not take you home with them. They do not take you to the book club or to work. So measure the thing that travels.

## Slide 16

Now repair. Three strategies, and they suit different populations. [pause] The first is shared context. Ask a caregiver to write down what their loved one says to the prompt, tell me about a day at the beach. Then run it again, but this time give both of them a picture of a beach scene to look at. [pause] Two things happen. The speaker's output gets richer, because they have something concrete to describe. And the listener can follow what is being pointed at and referred to. [pause] This one helps most when the breakdown is at the language level. Aphasia.

## Slide 17

The second is targeted repair. [pause] Instead of saying what, or asking the speaker to repeat the whole thing, the listener repeats back everything they did hear and leaves a blank where the gap was. You get, I heard you say did you get that from the, and then they stop. [pause] Now the speaker only has to repair one word instead of producing the entire utterance again. That is a very different amount of work, and it takes the burden off the person who is already working hardest. This one helps most in dysarthria. [pause] The third is topic marking. When a speaker starts, make a mental note of the topic. If they stall out, you can say, you were telling me about the grocery store. That is most useful in dementia. [pause] But offer repair carefully. A wrong guess creates a second breakdown stacked on top of the first. [pause] And remember this. Speech is redundant, but language is not. People who stutter will tell you that what they were about to say is almost never what someone finishing their sentence supplies.

## Slide 18

Last slide. [pause] Some of these conversations are hard, and you will have plenty of coursework ahead to build your counseling skills. But one thing has stayed true. [pause] It never hurts to ask the person directly. When would you like help. How would you like me to help. What should I avoid doing. What do you want this called, and is there language about your condition that feels wrong or upsetting to you. [pause] You can reframe things for buy-in without softening or obscuring the clinical facts. Those two are not in conflict. [pause] And this is the part I want to leave you with. That information is not in any research paper. You will not find it in a norms table or a protocol. It exists only in the person sitting in front of you, and finding it is part of the job of locating which link in their speech chain is breaking, and helping them repair it. [pause] Thank you. It has been a pleasure teaching you this semester.

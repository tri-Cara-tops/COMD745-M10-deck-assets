## Slide 3

Your reading this week is Chapter eleven of Denes and Pinson, Automatic Speech Recognition. [pause] The question that chapter opens with is the same question Module nine opened with. What in the acoustic signal identifies a sound? [pause] The answer people arrived at did not come from a computer. It came from building speech by hand. Researchers painted formant patterns onto film and played them back through a machine called the Pattern-Playback. If a hand-painted pattern is heard as a syllable, then whatever is in that painting is enough to specify the syllable.

## Slide 4

Here is what a painted pattern looks like next to a real spectrogram. [pause] On one side you have natural speech, dense and messy. On the other side, a few painted bars. Almost everything has been thrown away. [pause] And listeners still heard a syllable. The method is subtractive. Strip the signal down, play it, and if people still hear the sound, then what you left in is sufficient. Everything you removed was not necessary. That is how the field figured out which acoustic features actually carry consonant identity, decades before anyone could measure it automatically.

## Slide 5

Now the finding that matters most for this course. [pause] Look at the second formant. It does not start at a fixed frequency and sit there. It glides into the following vowel. That glide is called the second formant transition. [pause] Change the direction of that glide, or how far it travels, and the consonant people hear changes. Nothing else in the pattern moves. Same vowel, same duration, same everything. Only the transition. And the consonant changes.

## Slide 6

This is the figure I want you to hold onto. [pause] Six painted patterns. Played back, listeners hear pa, ta, ka across the top and ma, na, nga across the bottom. [pause] Now look at what the pairs share. Pa and ma have the same second formant transition. So do ta and na. So do ka and nga. Those pairs also share a place of articulation. Pa and ma are both made at the lips. Ta and na at the alveolar ridge. Ka and nga at the velum. [pause] So place of articulation lives in that transition. And the only thing separating the plosive from the nasal is what comes before the vowel: silence for the plosives, a low buzz for the nasals. [pause] Two features, two different parts of the signal, and both of them recoverable from patterns simple enough to paint by hand.

## Slide 7

One last thing before the next lecture. [pause] Denes and Pinson also show what happens when the signal is not enough on its own. Listeners resolve ambiguous sounds using the words around them. You already know this. You measured it on yourself in the Module nine lab, when your accuracy moved depending on how many answer choices were printed in front of you. [pause] The recognizer you are about to meet does not have that.
